Section 01

Living in a World of Highly Complex Technologies

Abstract

This opening section sets the stage for the site's central argument. As everyday technologies grow more sophisticated, humans no longer simply use them. We coexist with their implementation, their place in society, and their far reaching impact. Marvin traces this shift from simple tools such as a vacuum cleaner to complex systems that increasingly act as social partners, decision makers, or collaborators, including chatbots, agentic AI, and robotic systems. As interaction grows more complex, evaluating a technology means more than asking whether it works or feels good to use. It also means asking what the technology means for our lives and for society. This introduces the paradigm of User Perspectives, the idea that people hold both active and passive views of a technology at once.

The world and the technologies that surround us are becoming increasingly sophisticated, shaping nearly every aspect of daily life. Consequently, humans are no longer merely users of technological systems. We are forced to coexist with their implementation, their societal embedding, and their far-reaching impact on our environments. From the first moment of waking, when a smartphone serves as an alarm, through daily activities supported by smartwatches, to seemingly trivial routines such as brushing one's teeth with a connected device, technology accompanies us. It mediates social interaction, supports physical activity, structures routines, and increasingly defines how daily life unfolds. For relatively simple products, anticipating use was once straightforward. A technology designed to clean a floor, such as a vacuum, required reliable task performance, intuitive handling, and efficient execution. Interaction was limited in scope, the use case was clearly defined, and success could largely be assessed through functional outcomes. But interaction has expanded in its complexity and task-solving capabilities — for example, from sending brief messages between devices to engaging with systems that act as social partners. It has evolved from executing isolated calculations to supervising data-driven decision processes. And it has emerged from manually drilling a screw to lifting, rotating, and welding entire vehicle components through robotic systems. Human-technology interaction has thus shifted from hierarchical top-down support systems, such as customer support tools, toward complex technologies, such as customer support chatbots and agents, that partially or fully assume tasks themselves.

As complex technologies permeate everyday life, their impact intensifies accordingly. This fuels technocratic visions of a future in which automation enables humans to focus on what they value most, while raising dystopian concerns about the gradual erosion of the human role. Today, systems are no longer perceived merely as tools or functions but increasingly as companions, collaborators, or agents within the human environment. Whether technology is perceived as utopian or dystopian, whether evaluation focuses on handling or experience, one fundamental constant remains: the joint interaction between humans and technologies frames their relationship. While early technological systems could be sufficiently evaluated through system-centric design perspectives, sophisticated consumer technologies recognize the importance of experiential dimensions on top of basic functionality. At the same time, emerging complex technologies trigger humans' beliefs, emotions, and attitudes not only toward how technologies function but also toward their role in the world, their societal impact, and their implications for everyday life.

Situated within this context, I have positioned my research as a cognitive and behavioral psychologist investigating the interaction between humans and technologies. I examine how humans perceive, integrate, and reflect upon complex technologies that increasingly shape daily life. I believe that with the emerging complexity of technologies, humans integrate different perspectives of use into their representation and evaluation of a system. Whether they are actively controlling the system and are affected by its active use, or they are passively affected by the usage of a system without controlling it. I call this the paradigm of User Perspectives. This opens a new category of how users can relate to technologies from an interaction standpoint and forces us to evaluate technologies not only in regard to their immediate interaction with the system, but also to integrate a secondary layer of beliefs and attitudes that frame how we see the overall usage, impact, and relationship of this technological implementation within our environmental surroundings. This second layer looks beyond system design toward a contextual and impact-centric approach. This is grounded in the belief that we integrate different user perspectives as well as relations of impact and consequences into the acceptance, evaluation, and use behavior towards technologies. Making this measurable through the construct of Usage Perception helps not only to understand the interaction between a human and a technology more holistically, but also to frame and develop meaningful interaction with complex future technologies for every user.

Especially in the world of future automation, AI, and robotics, where our direct control diminishes but the daily life impact increases, we need to start creating active entry points that give not only the technology inventor but also the user the possibility to actively steer interactions beyond the ideas of system design. My research engages with this challenge and seeks to offer a useful theoretical, methodological, and practical answer.

Section 02

User Perspectives

Abstract

This section introduces the paradigm of User Perspectives, the distinction between actively controlling a technology and passively being affected by its use. In the past, interaction was active: people operated technologies fully, from vacuuming floors to driving cars. As systems automate more functions, such as robotic vacuums, driver assistance, and algorithmic social media, the balance shifts toward passive experience, where consequences arise even without direct control. Marvin argues this distinction concerns consequences rather than control alone. A driver actively operates a car, yet becomes a passive user when sharing a road with an autonomous vehicle. Both roles can occur at the same time. As interaction grows more passive, usability and experience become weaker predictors of acceptance, motivating a new evaluative layer called Usage Perception.

In the past, our interaction with technology was merely active: we controlled it to achieve a specific purpose under full control. When technologies emerged and became increasingly complex, this development shifted user perspectives: from an active user perspective toward an evolving passive one. More and more functions happen in the background, full operations became automated, and with the rising value of artificial intelligence and agentic AI, systems are not only taking over the job of reasoning and deciding, but also the role of applying and executing decisions in a human world. We are not predominantly controlling and executing the full interaction with the technology anymore, we are mostly affected by its use passively.

This started with simple technologies like the vacuum, where we were cleaning our homes ourselves, which is now fully done by an automatic robotic. But it also happens with complex technologies like the car, where in the past we as users were fully controlling the interaction and the driving behavior of the car, with all its complex cognitive and behavioral components. Today, this is partly done by ADAS systems, until fully assumed by autonomous driving cars, moving us from the very active position of a driver to someone in between, or in the end simply being a passenger. Additionally, it is not only changing technological interactions but also social systems, where we are replacing active interactions, such as talking and explaining our lives, emotions, and perceptions to other people, by using social media, AI-generated videos, or even replacing the human altogether in favour of a robot or artificial system. This results overall in a more passive mode of socialization, where for example reacting to very short content has become the primary mode over sharing mutual stories in social interaction, even if we are sitting next to each other.

The active-passive User Perspective is not primarily concerned with controllability. Its focus lies on the consequences and outcomes that arise from the changing interaction. Consider two examples. When driving a car, you are an active user, you control the system and are directly affected by how it performs. Sharing a road with autonomous vehicles, you are also a passive user at the same time, directly affected by its uncommon driving behavior, not being controlled by a human. The same logic applies in professional contexts. In an AI-driven HR decision system, the recruiter actively deploys the tool. The applicant, however, is passively subject to a data-driven judgment they neither initiated nor control. Both are users of the same technological process, differentiated only by their position within it. These relational structures emerge the moment a technology enters use. Both perspectives activate simultaneously, placing individuals in the position of being either actively, passively, or at the same time affected by both.

How visible this impact to the user is varies considerably. Sometimes we can anticipate the passive dimension early. Sometimes it surfaces through societal debate and emerging norms. As it is for example with social media, where the passive consequences of algorithmically curated content have gradually entered public consciousness, bringing widespread recognition of mounting self-perception issues. And sometimes the passive dimension remains entirely hidden at the point of adoption. As it is with the global deployment of large language models, whose consequences for labour markets and knowledge practices were largely unanticipated when the technology entered our societies.

My research builds upon this theory of User Perspectives. It examines how users evaluate the impact and contextual usage of a technology across active and passive interaction modes. This becomes particularly consequential as interaction grows increasingly passive. Under conditions of reduced user control, both the usability of a system and the role of user experience diminish as meaningful predictors of acceptance and behavior. What remains is an evaluative layer shaped by the effects and affects of technology use across these different perspectives. I argue that these user perspectives constitute a commonly existing, yet undertheorized, evaluative context. One that consequentially shapes how users relate to, accept, and ultimately engage with complex technology.

Whether or not users are aware of these different perspectives and their implications, they inevitably carry beliefs, attitudes, and evaluations toward a technology's integration, usage, and impact. In some cases, they are explicit and well-articulated. A user may consciously recognise that autonomous driving progressively reduces their driving-related competence. Or they may notice that algorithmically curated short-form content is eroding their capacity for sustained attention. An informed user reflecting on these dynamics would assess them as either beneficial or harmful passive effects of technology use. In other cases, however, no such clarity exists. Users hold diffuse impressions, vague anxieties, or unexamined intuitions about why they accept or resist a particular technology.

For example, autonomous vehicles illustrate this precisely. Empirical evidence consistently shows that widespread adoption would substantially reduce road fatalities. Yet public apprehension persists, not because the data is unknown, but because the technology carries a felt relevance that resists purely rational resolution. This discrepancy is not an anomaly. Even where a technology is judged as usable, useful, and enjoyable, the evaluation of its broader implementation and contextual usage may simultaneously be more critical, more ambivalent, or more resistant, which is considered by the theory of layered attitudes and the construct of Usage Perception.

Section 03

Layered Attitudes

Abstract

This section presents the theory of layered attitudes, the idea that people evaluate technologies on two distinct levels. The first layer concerns system design: how usable, useful, and enjoyable a technology feels to use. The second, often overlooked layer concerns usage and impact: what it means for a technology to enter a specific context, and what consequences follow for the user, their environment, and society. Using examples such as industrial robots, automated grading systems, and social robots, Marvin shows how these layers can diverge sharply for the very same technology. This contextual layer is named Usage Perception. The section argues that as interaction becomes more passive, Usage Perception increasingly outweighs conventional usability judgments in shaping how people relate to a technology.

As the impact of complex technologies increases and affects us intensively in our day-to-day lives, we evaluate them across different layers of attitudes. At first, we might think that a system is very useful, that its usability is great, and that interacting with it is genuinely pleasant. These judgments lead to positive evaluations of a technology's system design and interaction quality. They form the first attitudinal layer: how we feel about the technology itself. But complex technologies do not only affect us during active use. They affect our environment, our society, and the broader world in which they are embedded. We ask: what happens when this technology is integrated into a specific context? What does its usage mean for us as users, and for those around us? This is the second layer of attitudes towards the system usage and impact.

An industrial robot introduced into a workplace may be easy to operate, offer a good interaction experience, and free up time for more meaningful tasks. From a system design perspective, the evaluation is positive. But in the specific context of an existing job, the same technology may lead to role displacement, the need to reskill, and a sense of loss, a deeply negative contextual evaluation of the very same system's impact. Automated grading systems in schools may be useful and technically well-designed. From an interaction standpoint, they might even be enjoyable. But looking at the contextual impact, both users and decision-makers may hold very different attitudes. Does the system harm the relationship between teacher and student? Does it free up time to build meaningful human connections, or does it substitute for them? The system design evaluation and the usage evaluation diverge. A well-designed, usable, and enjoyable social robot may be perceived very differently depending on who is using it and in what context. For a socially active young person, its integration as a social companion might feel harmful and unnecessary. For an elderly or isolated person, the same robot fulfils a genuine need for interaction and companionship. The technology is identical. The attitudinal, contextual evaluation of the usage is not.

I define this, in general terms, as layered attitudes. We hold attitudes toward the system design of a technology; how easy it is to use and how enjoyable the interaction is. At the same time, we hold distinct attitudes toward its usage and implementation: a summative and abstract evaluation of the technology's impact on us as users, on our environment, and on the social systems we inhabit. This is the second attitudinal layer, I introduced as Usage Perception, the attitude toward the context-specific and user-bound impact of a technology's usage.

This distinction becomes even more pronounced when viewed through the lens of the different User Perspectives outlined earlier. In an active user perspective, for example, someone directly operating a data-driven decision system, the layered attitudes take one shape. In the passive user perspective, for example, someone merely being affected by that same system, they take another. When both perspectives are simultaneously present, these layers interact and compound. The more passive the interaction with a technology becomes, the more important Usage Perception will be. In the absence of direct control, abstract evaluations of impact and consequence increasingly define the relationship between user and technology beyond the system design.

My research considers Usage Perception not simply as an additional explanatory layer. It is a conceptual entry point, that connects individual interaction with broader questions of impact, societal values, governance, and legal accountability. At a practical and user accessible scale. It also bridges the divide between active and passive user perspectives, allowing us to explain the full range of a user's relationship with a technology. This has direct consequences for the design of technologies: technologies developed with Usage Perception in mind are better suited to the actual needs, concerns, and values of their users. And it gives the possibility to frame and learn an active entry point towards the interaction with technologies.

In a future where technologies become hyper-individualized, personally configured, and deeply embedded in everyday contexts, the role of traditional usability and user experience metrics will continue to diminish. What will matter more is how users evaluate the impact, consequences, and meaning of a technology's presence in their lives. Focusing on layered attitudes and Usage Perception gives both researchers and users a new and necessary entry point. One that mastering may well become an essential skillset in navigating increasingly technocratic futures.

Section 04

The Construct of Usage Perception

Abstract

This section formally introduces Usage Perception, a construct capturing the summative, abstract attitudinal evaluation of a technology's overall usage and broader impact, beyond system design. Unlike Perceived Usefulness, Usability, or User Experience, Usage Perception looks outward: what does using this technology mean, and what does it do to the user, their environment, and the world? It is defined as the degree to which someone evaluates a technology's overall usage and impact as positive or negative, distinct from judgments about interface quality or momentary interaction. The section introduces its measurement instrument, a semantic differential scale of seven bipolar adjective pairs, extendable with up to three additional items. It closes by framing Usage Perception as a validated entry point for understanding technology evaluation as systems grow more complex.

Usage Perception is a construct I developed to capture what happens beyond the system design. It is defined as a summative and abstract attitudinal evaluation of a technology's overall usage and its broader impact. This goes beyond evaluating whether a technology is easy to use, useful, or enjoyable. It asks a fundamentally different question. What does it mean to use this technology? And what does its use do to us, our environment, and the world around us?

To understand what makes Usage Perception distinct, it helps to briefly distinguish it from constructs that already exist. Perceived Usefulness asks whether a technology helps us achieve a specific goal. Usability asks whether it is easy to operate. User Experience asks how the interaction feels emotionally and aesthetically. Usage Perception looks outward. It captures the broader, more abstract attitude a user holds toward the technology as a whole, its contextual implementation and impact. This includes its role in their life, its consequences for others, and its place in society.

More precisely, UP represents a summative and abstract yet cognitively weighted attitudinal evaluation of a technology's broader impact and usage beyond its immediate system design. Unlike system related attitudes that address interface quality, task support, or experiential engagement, UP transcends immediate interaction to encompass judgments about what a technology means for the user's life, environment, and broader context. It encompasses not only active interaction but also passive forms of influence and exposure. Furthermore, UP is specific, directed, and context bound toward the usage and impact of a technology. Formally, it is defined as the degree to which an individual evaluates the overall usage and broader impact of a specific technology within its implementation context as positive or negative, distinct from evaluations of its interface design or the quality of momentary interaction.

Consistent with attitude theory emphasizing that attitudes balance persistence with adaptability, UP demonstrates temporal stability while remaining responsive to meaningful experiential variation. This hybrid character renders the construct suitable for capturing evaluative orientations that evolve over time. Rather than replacing system focused evaluations, UP complements them by adding a theoretically grounded layer that addresses the full scope of attitudes toward complex technologies.

Usage Perception is measured through a semantic differential scale. At its core, it consists of seven bipolar adjective pairs. Users are asked to evaluate the technology using items such as harmful/beneficial, undesirable/desirable, bad/good, irritating/likeable, unpleasant/pleasant, unfavourable/favourable, and negative/positive. The guiding question is: "Overall, I perceive this technology, its impact and usage as:". The scale can be extended by up to three additional context specific items, depending on the technology and research setting. This modular design allows the construct to be applied flexibly across different domains while maintaining a stable and comparable core.

In short, Usage Perception gives researchers and practitioners a validated and measurable entry point into the part of technology evaluation that matters as systems grow more complex: the second layer of attitudes. It captures not just how users feel about a system, but how they judge the role that system plays in the world, and it can integrate different user perspectives. It offers a theoretically valuable framework grounded in cognitive psychological research models, which translates into behavioral psychological models, paralleling the triad of emotion, cognition, and behavior. This reveals UP's full scope and value: stabilizing technology acceptance models across different technologies and user perspectives while still accounting for their systematic differences. It presents a streamlined, empirically supported extension for explaining behavior both stably and comprehensively. In doing so, it integrates a summative and abstract evaluative layer that captures the directive impacts of technology usage within the adoption process, thereby enabling a more holistic explanation of the full scope of human-technology interaction. Furthermore, UP demonstrates motivational relevance by predicting actual use. At the same time, it remains broadly reactive yet stable across interaction modalities, user perspectives, and time, while preserving its reflective and evaluative character.

Section 04.1

Measuring Usage Perception

Abstract

This subsection details how Usage Perception is measured. The core scale consists of seven semantic differential items, such as Harmful and Beneficial, Bad and Good, and Negative and Positive, each rated on a six point scale in response to the prompt: Overall, I perceive this technology, its impact and usage as. All seven items should be administered together as a single construct, and removing items without revalidation is discouraged. The scale can be extended with up to three additional items suited to a given technology or setting, such as risky and safe in autonomous driving research. Items may only be excluded for clear theoretical reasons, with a strict limit of two, after which reliability must be rechecked.

The Usage Perception scale consists of a stable core of seven semantic differential items. Each item presents two opposing adjectives at either end of a six point scale. Users position their evaluation somewhere between the two poles. The question that frames all items is: "Overall, I perceive this technology, its impact and usage as:".

The seven core items are:

  • 1Harmful / Beneficial
  • 2Undesirable / Desirable
  • 3Bad / Good
  • 4Irritating / Likeable
  • 5Unpleasant / Pleasant
  • 6Unfavourable / Favourable
  • 7Negative / Positive

All seven items should always be administered together. They form a single, internally consistent construct. Removing or replacing individual items without re-validation is not recommended.

Depending on the research context, the core scale can be extended by up to three additional items. These context specific items capture attitudes that are particularly relevant to a given technology or setting. In an autonomous driving study, for example, items such as risky/safe or suspicious/trustworthy may be added. In a human robot interaction study, items such as inappropriate/appropriate or deceitful/social may be more fitting. These additional items do not replace the core. They complement it.

The extended scale can be modelled in two ways. As a single factor, treating all items as reflections of one underlying construct. Or as a two factor model, separating the core items from the context specific additions. Both approaches are empirically supported. The choice depends on the goals of the study.

Not all seven core items must be retained in every application. When individual items drastically fail to meet common loading thresholds, exclusion is appropriate. Items should only be excluded when there is a clear theoretical and contextual justification rooted in the technology or implementation context. Exclusion decisions must be theory and research driven. Exploratory item removal without substantive justification is not appropriate. A maximum of two core items may be excluded in any single application. Excluding more than two items risks compromising the construct's representational coverage. When items are excluded, reliability must be rechecked before proceeding with analysis.

Usage Perception integrates both emotional and cognitive evaluations into a single attitudinal judgment. This means the construct will naturally correlate with emotional, cognitive, and other attitudinal measures. High correlations with such measures do not constitute a problem in themselves. As long as the inclusion of Usage Perception meaningfully increases explained variability and correlations remain statistically significant, the measure is contributing unique information to the model. Researchers should evaluate the construct's contribution in terms of incremental explanatory value rather than treating intercorrelations with related constructs as a disqualifying concern.

Section 04.2

Calculating Usage Perception

Abstract

This subsection explains how to score Usage Perception. Each of the seven items is rated on a six point scale, where higher scores indicate a more positive perception. For simple analyses, a composite score can be calculated as the mean across all seven items. For more rigorous work, Usage Perception is best modeled as a latent construct within a structural equation model, an approach that accounts for measurement error and yields more stable estimates. Before analysis, reliability should be checked. Across three validation studies, the core scale showed strong internal consistency, with reliability values consistently above ninety percent. If additional items are used, their relationship to the core construct should be examined first through a confirmatory factor analysis.

Each item is scored on a six point scale. The left pole represents the most negative evaluation and the right pole the most positive. Higher scores therefore indicate a more positive Usage Perception toward the technology.

For a basic composite score, calculate the mean across all seven core items. This single number represents a user's overall Usage Perception. It can be used directly in descriptive analyses and simple comparisons.

For structural or inferential analyses, Usage Perception is best treated as a latent construct within a structural equation model. In this case, the seven items serve as reflective indicators of the underlying construct. This approach accounts for measurement error and produces more stable and reliable estimates. Both partial least squares and covariance based structural equation modelling are appropriate, depending on sample size and study design.

Before using the scale in analysis, reliability should be verified. Across three independent validation studies, the core scale consistently demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega both above .90 and Average Variance Extracted above .67. Results falling substantially below these benchmarks may indicate issues with item comprehension, translation, or fit to the specific technology context.

If the optional context specific items are included, their relationship to the core construct should be examined before analysis. A two factor confirmatory factor analysis, separating core from context specific items, provides a useful starting point.

Section 05

For Researchers

Abstract

This section offers guidance for researchers who want to use Usage Perception and layered attitudes in their own work. The construct is designed to integrate into existing research rather than replace it, and applies across technology domains, study designs, and disciplines. As a standalone measure, the brief and validated scale can be added to any study without structural changes. Within structural equation modeling, Usage Perception integrates naturally alongside established frameworks and tends to improve model stability across technologies and settings. It can also be used simply, through its mean composite score, suiting smaller samples. Finally, the section urges researchers to define user groups along the active to passive continuum before data collection, treating this distinction as theoretically meaningful.

Usage Perception and the framework of layered attitudes are designed to be integrated into existing research rather than replacing it. This section offers guidance for researchers who want to incorporate these ideas into their work. The construct applies across technology domains, study designs, and analytical approaches. It is relevant wherever technology evaluation is part of the research question. It can be used to study how users perceive a technology, to inform design decisions, and to investigate the character and quality of human-technology interaction. It equally supports the examination of a technology's broader impact, its societal values, and the attitudinal consequences of its deployment. This breadth of application makes UP relevant across disciplines. Its integration is possible from engineering and design through the social and behavioral sciences and into the natural sciences.

UP as a Standalone Construct

Usage Perception can be integrated into any technology-related study as a standalone measure. No modification to an existing study design is required. The semantic differential scale is self contained, brief, and validated. It can be administered alongside existing questionnaires without structural changes to the study. In quantitative designs, UP adds an attitudinal dimension that existing constructs do not cover. In qualitative designs, the construct offers a theoretical lens for interpreting user narratives about technology impact and broader meaning.

Integration into Structural Models

For researchers working with structural equation models, UP integrates naturally as a latent construct. It can be added to established frameworks such as TAM or UTAUT without replacing their existing components. UP-integrated models consistently demonstrate greater structural stability across different technologies and interaction settings. This is a direct methodological benefit. When findings diverge across studies or technology contexts, researchers should consider whether the attitudinal domain has been fully specified before attributing inconsistency to methodological factors or sample characteristics alone. Both partial least squares and covariance based structural equation modelling are appropriate. The choice depends on sample size and study design.

Simple Statistical Applications

Structural equation modelling is not required. UP can also be used as a simple summative construct. The mean across the seven core items represents a user's overall Usage Perception as a single score. This score is directly usable in descriptive statistics, group comparisons, regression analyses, and correlational studies. Researchers with smaller samples or simpler designs can still benefit from the construct without adopting a full structural modelling approach.

Addressing User Perspectives

One of the consequential dimensions in complex technology research is the distinction between active and passive user perspectives. Active users directly control a technology. Passive users are affected by its use without controlling it. This distinction is not a mere contextual variation. It introduces structural differences in how attitudes form and how constructs relate to one another.

Researchers are advised to define user groups along the active to passive continuum before data collection begins. These groups should be treated as theoretically meaningful conditions rather than as control variables. Comparing active and passive users on UP and related constructs reveals how the evaluative process shifts depending on a user's role and degree of control. This is particularly relevant for technologies where active and passive usage modes coexist by design, such as autonomous systems, AI assisted workflows, or shared digital environments.

Section 06

For Practitioners

Abstract

This section turns Usage Perception into a practical tool for designers, engineers, product managers, marketers, and innovators, as well as for users themselves, who gain language for reflecting on their own relationship with technology. It argues that traditional usability and experience research, while necessary, is not sufficient for complex technologies, since users also judge a technology's purpose and consequences, which can diverge sharply from system design evaluations. Bringing Usage Perception into existing research reveals adoption barriers early, before launch. The section also stresses addressing the full range of user perspectives, not only direct operators but affected stakeholders who shape organizational decisions. For highly individualized technologies, Usage Perception offers a stable reference point that transcends individual configurations, useful at every stage of development.

Usage Perception is not only a tool for researchers. It is a practical instrument for everyone involved in bringing technology to people. Designers, engineers, product managers, marketers, and innovators all stand to benefit from understanding how users evaluate a technology's broader impact and usage. But the construct is also meaningful for users themselves. It gives them a language and a framework to reflect on their own relationship with technology. It returns a degree of control and self-awareness to the user.

Extending Beyond Usability and Experience

Traditional user research focuses on system design. It asks whether the interface works, whether the experience is satisfying, and whether the technology is useful for its intended task. These are important questions. They are not sufficient on their own. For complex technologies, users also form evaluative judgments about a technology's purpose, consequences, and broader role in their lives. These judgments can diverge from system design evaluations. A product may score well on usability and user experience while users simultaneously hold unfavorable views of what that technology does in the world.

Integrating UP into existing user research processes makes this divergence visible early. Unfavorable usage perceptions identified before launch signal adoption barriers that interface iteration alone cannot resolve. Understanding these perceptions is not supplementary work. It is constitutive of a genuinely human centered design approach. A design process that optimizes system properties while leaving unexamined how users evaluate the technology's purpose and consequences is incomplete by definition.

Addressing the Full Range of User Perspectives

Most user research focuses on direct operators. This misses a consequential portion of the evaluative landscape. For many technologies, affected stakeholders who do not directly control the system are equally relevant to adoption success. Their perceptions influence organizational decisions, social legitimacy, and real world uptake.

Practitioners should define the full continuum of user perspectives from the outset. This means identifying not only who operates the technology but also who is affected by its use. In organizational contexts, communication strategies should explicitly address what the technology does, what consequences it may carry, and how its implementation aligns with organizational and social values. Development and evaluation processes should represent this full range of perspectives from an early stage. User perspective should not be treated as a fixed classification. It is a dynamic property of the interaction context, to be examined across the range of realistic deployment conditions.

Highly Individualized Technologies

The relevance of UP increases in proportion to technological complexity. This is especially true for highly individualized systems, where the interface adapts dynamically to each user's behavior, preferences, and interaction history. In these environments, system design evaluations become increasingly unstable. What one user experiences at the interface level may differ substantially from what another experiences. Standard usability and experience comparisons become difficult to interpret across these variations.

Usage Perception offers a stable reference point precisely because it transcends individual interface configurations. It captures the user's overall evaluative orientation toward what the technology does and what it means. Even as interface conditions vary, this higher level attitudinal evaluation remains comparable across users and contexts. Practitioners benefit from assessing not only whether users are satisfied with their individual interaction but also whether users share a coherent and favorable orientation toward the technology as a whole. Small changes in a system's overall purpose or design can destabilize evaluation models entirely. Attending to both layers simultaneously provides a more complete and robust picture of how users relate to the technology.

A Tool That Belongs at Every Stage

UP is applicable from early concept testing through post launch evaluation. It requires no special infrastructure and fits into existing survey designs. It produces results that translate directly into actionable insights for design, communication, and strategy. Whether the goal is to understand adoption barriers, to evaluate the success of an intervention, or to compare how different user groups relate to the same technology, UP offers a validated and efficient entry point into the evaluative dimension that complex technologies demand.
